\title{Consistency by Construction: Cross-Encoders with Reversal-Aware Decoding
for SemEval-2027 Task~2 (DiCo-NLI)}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Le Duc Minh}
\IEEEauthorblockA{minh.leduc.0210@gmail.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
DiCo-NLI asks a system to label an ordered phrase pair as equivalence, forward
entailment, backward entailment or another relation, and then checks whether
the labels it gives to $(a,b)$ and $(b,a)$ agree. We fine-tune multilingual
cross-encoders and move consistency out of training and into decoding: the
log-probabilities of a pair and its reversed twin are mapped into one frame,
averaged, and decoded once, so every pair predicted as reversible is
consistent by construction. Almost all remaining errors lie in deciding whether
a pair is in an entailment relation at all. Six methods aimed at that decision,
including focal loss, extra iSTS and WordNet data and a stacked classifier,
did not beat a plain ensemble. What did help is a hierarchical composition:
ordinary four-way models decide the negative class, and models trained only on
the three entailment labels choose among them. Averaged over the English,
Spanish, Basque and mixed tracks of the development set, this raises Weighted
F1 / SoftCons / HardCons from 80.8 / 95.7 / 88.0 to 81.4 / 96.8 / 89.5 with fixed
members, and to 83.7 / 98.6 / 91.7 with members selected per track. If the
reversible/negative split is read from the layout of the input files, a
selected ensemble reaches 94.3 / 100 / 93.1, above the current development
leader (91.4 / 100 / 89.7). We report that variant separately because it
depends on how the data were packaged.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Natural language inference (NLI) models are usually scored one example at a
time. A model can do well on that score and still contradict itself, for
example by labelling both $(a,b)$ and $(b,a)$ as forward entailment, which
would make each phrase strictly more specific than the other. SemEval-2027 Task~2, DiCo-NLI~\cite{dicoNLIsite,dicoNLIrepo}, makes
this visible. Every reversible phrase pair appears in both orders, and systems
are scored on per-example Weighted F1 and on two consistency measures over the
pairs.

Our system is built around one observation: consistency can be enforced at
decoding time for free, because both directions of every reversible pair are
in the same input file. We average the two directions' scores in a shared
frame and choose a single label for the pair. This leaves the classifier with
one job, which is to separate the entailment family from everything else.
That job turned out to be the hard part. This report describes the decoding
scheme and two exactly reversal-equivariant model variants; compares seven
backbones, including two multilingual rerankers; profiles where the errors come
from; and reports a negative result, since none of the six \NEG-focused
methods we tried improved on a plain ensemble. The method that did help reuses
models trained without the negative class inside a hierarchical composition,
and per-track ensemble selection adds more on the development set. Code, configurations and run logs are
public.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/MinLee0210/dico-nli-challenge}}

\section{Task and Data}

Each instance is an ordered pair $(a,b)$ of short phrases, about six words in
total, taken from the chunk alignments of PhrasIS~\cite{phrasis2024}, which in
turn come from the interpretable STS datasets of SemEval
2015--2016~\cite{semeval2015sts,semeval2016ists,lopezgazpio2017ists}. The label
is \EQ, \FE{} ($a \models b$), \BE{} ($b \models a$) or \NEG, where \NEG{}
covers every other PhrasIS relation (similar, related, opposite). The reversal
map is $\rev(\EQ)=\EQ$, $\rev(\FE)=\BE$ and $\rev(\BE)=\FE$; \NEG{} has no
reverse. There are four tracks: English (T1), Spanish (T2), Basque (T3) and
mixed-language pairs (T4).

Weighted F1 is the support-weighted F1 over the four labels. SoftCons counts
a gold-reversible pair as consistent when both predictions are reversible and
$\rev$-compatible, whatever the gold label. HardCons requires both directions
to be correct. Predicting \NEG{} on either side of a gold-reversible pair
therefore costs both consistency scores at once, which makes a false \NEG{}
the most expensive error.

We checked three facts on every training and development file. Instance ids
have the form \texttt{<pair\_id>\_\_<l1>-<l2>\_\_<original|flipped>}, and the
reversed twin swaps both the language codes and the direction tag. Every
reversible instance has its twin in the same file, while every \NEG{} instance
appears once. A \texttt{pair\_id} is shared across tracks, with two rows in
each monolingual track and twelve in T4. Each monolingual track has 3{,}042
training rows (1{,}282 reversible pairs and 478 \NEG) and 660 development rows
(277 and 106).

Table~\ref{tab:profile} splits the English pairs by how their words overlap,
which turns out to predict where models go wrong. When the words of one phrase
are contained in the other (\emph{in a field}/\emph{in a huge field}), the pair
is almost always directional: 418 of 458
training pairs are \FE{} or \BE{} and only 13 are \NEG. When the phrases share
no word at all, \EQ{} (142) and \NEG{} (114) dominate, and telling them apart
needs lexical knowledge: \emph{climbs}/\emph{moves up} is \EQ{}, while
\emph{along a runway}/\emph{on the tarmac} is \NEG. Most \NEG{} pairs (351 of
478) fall in the middle group, where the phrases share some words and differ in
others (\emph{on Monday}/\emph{on Thursday}, \emph{across the sand}/\emph{through
the water}). Only 43 training pairs contain two different numbers, so numeric
mismatch is a minor error source. Fig.~\ref{fig:profile} shows the label mix of
each group.

\section{Related Work}

Interpretable STS~\cite{lopezgazpio2017ists,semeval2016ists} introduced the
chunk-level relation types from which PhrasIS and DiCo-NLI are derived.
Consistency of neural models under logical transformations has been studied
for NLI~\cite{minervini2018adversarially,li2019logic}, usually through extra
loss terms. For
symmetric pair tasks, Xu et al.~\cite{xu2019symmetric} and Kumar and
Joshi~\cite{kumar2022striking} penalise disagreement between the two input
orders; we use the same idea for an antisymmetric label set, where the two
orders should agree up to the $\rev$ map. Our \NEG{} study draws on work
showing that NLI models lean on lexical overlap~\cite{hans2019} and fail on
simple lexical inferences~\cite{glockner2018breaking}, and on focal
loss~\cite{lin2017focal} and intermediate-task transfer~\cite{pruksachatkun2020intermediate}
as remedies. For Basque we rely on multilingual encoders; XLM-R-large was the
strongest of the multilingual encoders evaluated on XNLIeu~\cite{xnlieu2024}.
Lexical knowledge in NLI has often come from WordNet~\cite{miller1995wordnet};
the Open Multilingual Wordnet~\cite{bond2013omw} and the Multilingual Central
Repository~\cite{gonzalezagirre2012mcr} link Spanish and Basque wordnets to the
same synsets, which we use to build training pairs for all three languages.
Beyond NLI checkpoints, we try two multilingual rerankers of a different
pretraining lineage, BGE-M3's reranker~\cite{chen2024bgem3} and the Qwen3
reranker~\cite{qwen3embedding2025}. Our ensembles use greedy forward selection
from a library of models~\cite{caruana2004ensemble}.

\section{System}

\subsection{Cross-encoder}
A pretrained encoder reads \texttt{[CLS]}~$a$~\texttt{[SEP]}~$b$~\texttt{[SEP]}
and a linear head maps the first-token state to four
logits~\cite{bert2019,wolf2020transformers}. We use
DeBERTa-v3~\cite{deberta2021,debertav3} for English only, and three
multilingual backbones: mmBERT-base~\cite{mmbert2025}, an mDeBERTa-v3-base
checkpoint fine-tuned on multilingual NLI data~\cite{laurer2023universal},
and XLM-R-large~\cite{xlmr2020} fine-tuned on XNLI~\cite{xnli2018,multinli2018}.
Multilingual models are trained on all four tracks at once (27{,}378 rows),
so each source pair is seen in nine language combinations.

\subsection{Consistency-aware decoding}
Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} shows the decoding pipeline. For an instance $x$ with
log-probabilities $\ell(x)\in\mathbb{R}^4$, the canonical score is
$\tilde\ell(x)=\ell(x)$ for \texttt{original} rows and $\tilde\ell(x)=P\,\ell(x)$
for \texttt{flipped} rows, where the permutation $P$ swaps the \FE{} and \BE{}
coordinates. Instances are grouped and the mean of $\tilde\ell$ over a group
is decoded to one canonical label, after adding a bias $\beta$ to the \NEG{}
score:
\begin{equation}
  y^\ast = \arg\max_{c}\; \Bigl(\tfrac{1}{|G|}\textstyle\sum_{x\in G}\tilde\ell_c(x)
  + \beta\,[c=\NEG]\Bigr).
\end{equation}
Original rows receive $y^\ast$ and flipped rows receive $\rev(y^\ast)$. With
\emph{independent} grouping each instance is its own group, which is plain
argmax. \emph{Twin} grouping joins an instance with its reversed twin.
\emph{Source} grouping joins every row with the same \texttt{pair\_id} in the
decoded file, which pools language views in T4. Any group holding both
directions is $\rev$-consistent by construction. All per-track results in this
report decode each track file on its own.

\subsection{Symmetric architecture}
Decoding gives consistency only when both directions are in the input. We
also built a model that is consistent for any single input. It scores both
orders with the same encoder $f$ and combines them as
\begin{equation}
  g(a,b) = \tfrac12\bigl(f(a,b) + P\,f(b,a)\bigr),
\end{equation}
so $g(b,a)=P\,g(a,b)$ holds exactly (Fig.~\ref{fig:symmetric}). The cost is
two forward passes per example. A unit test checks the identity to within
$10^{-5}$.

\subsection{Entailment-factorized head}
\label{sec:factorized}
The flat head throws away the 3-way NLI head of the checkpoints we start from.
The four DiCo labels are, however, the four combinations of two entailment
decisions, so we also built a head that keeps it. With
$q=p(\text{entailment}\mid a,b)$ and $q'=p(\text{entailment}\mid b,a)$ read from
the unchanged NLI head, the label distribution is
\begin{align}
  p(\EQ) &= q\,q', & p(\FE) &= q\,(1-q'), \nonumber\\
  p(\BE) &= (1-q)\,q', & p(\NEG) &= (1-q)(1-q').
\end{align}
The four terms sum to one, swapping $a$ and $b$ swaps $q$ and $q'$ and hence
\FE{} and \BE, so the head is exactly reversal-equivariant like the symmetric
model, and it starts from a working entailment classifier instead of a random
layer. Training minimises cross-entropy on the composed distribution; the
consistency term is zero by construction and is switched off.



\subsection{Training objectives}
The loss is cross-entropy, optionally with label smoothing,
plus a reversal-consistency term between each row and its twin, which a
twin-aware batch sampler keeps in the same batch:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathrm{CE} + \lambda\,\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{sym}}\bigl(p(x)\,\|\,P\,p(x')\bigr).
\end{equation}
For the \NEG{} study we added class-weighted and focal~\cite{lin2017focal}
variants of the cross-entropy term and a \emph{reverse-negative} augmentation
that adds the reversed copy of each \NEG{} pair; \NEG{} is its own reverse, so
the label stays correct.

\subsection{Structural prior}
The second data fact makes the reversible-or-\NEG{} decision readable from the
file: a group whose reversed twin is present is decoded among \EQ, \FE{} and
\BE, and a group without a twin is decoded as \NEG. This is exact on training
and development data. It is a property of how the files were built, not of
the phrases, and the test files may be packaged differently, so we report
every result with and without it. \emph{Prior-aware} models are trained on the
three reversible labels only, since under the prior the model is never asked
about \NEG; they must not be used without it.

\subsection{Hierarchical composition}
\label{sec:hier}
Prior-aware models turn out to be better at choosing among \EQ, \FE{} and \BE{}
than models that also learn \NEG, because none of their capacity goes to the
fourth class. We reuse them without the prior by composing two ensembles
(Fig.~\ref{fig:hier}). A \emph{gate} ensemble of ordinary four-way models gives
$g=p(\NEG)$, and a \emph{direction} ensemble of prior-aware models gives a
distribution $q$ over the three reversible labels:
\begin{equation}
  p(\NEG)=g,\qquad p(c)=(1-g)\,q(c),\quad c\in\{\EQ,\FE,\BE\}.
\end{equation}
The composed scores go through the same consistency-aware decoding as any
other model. Nothing here reads the file layout: the gate decides \NEG{} from
the phrases alone.

\subsection{Lexical-relation data from WordNet}
\label{sec:wordnet}
To give the models the hypernym-versus-sibling knowledge that the error
analysis (Section~\ref{sec:errors}) points to, we generate short pairs from the
Open Multilingual Wordnet~\cite{bond2013omw} through the \texttt{wn}
library~\cite{goodman2021wn}. English, Spanish and Basque synsets share
interlingual identifiers, so one English hypernym graph labels all three
languages. A hyponym with its direct hypernym gives \FE{} (\emph{creek}
$\models$ \emph{stream}), with the reversed row as \BE; two lemmas of one
synset give \EQ; two siblings under the same hypernym give \NEG{}
(\emph{creek}/\emph{river}), written once as DiCo writes \NEG{} pairs. We draw
600 pairs per label and language, 30\% of them with the two phrases in
different languages as in T4, for 9{,}000 rows in total, and add them to the
training data. The lemmas come from the whole wordnet, so many are rarer than
the caption and headline vocabulary of DiCo.

\subsection{Ensemble selection}
\label{sec:selection}
With about forty trained models, we choose ensemble members by greedy forward
selection~\cite{caruana2004ensemble}: start from the best single model and
repeatedly add, with replacement, the model that most raises the mean of the
three official scores, stopping when no addition helps. Each track is ranked on
its own, so we select a separate ensemble per track, which also lets T1 use
English-only models. For the hierarchical system, each step may add a model to
either the gate or the direction ensemble. Selection uses the development
labels, so selected scores are optimistic estimates of test performance; we
report fixed ensembles alongside them.

All models were trained on one NVIDIA RTX~4000~Ada (20\,GB) with mixed
precision, AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled}, 10\% linear warm-up and linear
decay, batch size 16--32 and maximum length 64--128 subword tokens. Learning
rates were $2\cdot10^{-5}$ for DeBERTa-v3-base, $5\cdot10^{-5}$ for mmBERT,
$1.5\cdot10^{-5}$ for mDeBERTa and $10^{-5}$ for XLM-R-large. The symmetric
XLM-R model used batch size 8 to fit in memory. The multilingual models reach
their best score within the first epoch, so for the main runs we validate
every 15\% of an epoch and stop after five checks without improvement. The
factorized-head runs (Section~\ref{sec:factorized}) used the same settings on
one NVIDIA L4 (24\,GB), and the reranker, WordNet and later prior-aware runs on
one NVIDIA A40 (48\,GB), up to four at a time. bge-reranker-v2-m3 uses a
learning rate of $10^{-5}$; Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B, a decoder, reads the pair
through a short prompt (\emph{Phrase A: \ldots\ Phrase B: \ldots\ Relation:})
with bf16 autocast and a learning rate of $2\cdot10^{-5}$, and is classified
from its last token. We keep every model at or below about 600M parameters.
All runs are logged to Weights \& Biases.

The early-stopping monitor is the mean of the three official scores with the
four development files decoded together. It is only used to stop training and
to compare runs quickly; every table decodes each track separately. Ensembles
average log-probabilities before decoding.
Our metric code agrees with the official scorer to machine precision on all
tracks and decoding modes, and the headline results were re-scored with the
official scorer.

\subsection{Backbones and model variants}
Tables~\ref{tab:clean} and~\ref{tab:prior} give per-track development scores
without and with the structural prior. The two NLI-trained encoders,
mDeBERTa and XLM-R-large, are close to each other and well ahead of mmBERT,
which starts from masked-language-model weights and overfits after one epoch.
A Basque-only encoder trained on T3 alone (JaunBERT) reached a monitor score
of only 76.9, so we kept the multilingual models for Basque. The symmetric
architecture does not help a single model, and adding it to the clean
ensemble lowers HardCons from 88.0 to 87.3. With consistency already enforced
at decoding time, the architectural guarantee adds nothing here.

On T1, the English-only DeBERTa-v3-large reaches 83.3 / 95.3 / 88.1, but the
multilingual ensemble already scores 83.7 / 95.3 / 89.9 and adding the large
model does not change it. Seed and backbone ensembles give the only
consistent gains. The best clean system averages seven models (four mDeBERTa
and three XLM-R-large seeds) and reaches 80.8 / 95.7 / 88.0 over the four
tracks.

With the prior, the prior-aware models are the strongest single models, and
the best system averages fourteen of them. It reaches 92.6 / 100 / 91.2 over
the four tracks, above the development leader (91.4 / 100 / 89.7) on T1, T3,
T4 and the average, and slightly below it on T2.

The two rerankers, added for diversity, are weaker than the NLI checkpoints.
bge-reranker-v2-m3 reaches 75.7 / 96.2 / 82.6 without the prior, three to four
F1 points below mDeBERTa and XLM-R-large. Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B does worse still
(62.6 F1): it almost never predicts \NEG, which gives it the highest SoftCons in
Table~\ref{tab:clean} and the lowest F1. With the prior both stay behind the
NLI encoders, so relevance-ranking pretraining does not transfer well to
this label set.

The WordNet pairs of Section~\ref{sec:wordnet} move F1 in the right direction
for mDeBERTa (80.0 against 79.4) at a cost in consistency (93.4 SoftCons
against 95.9), and hurt XLM-R-large on every metric. In the prior-aware models
they help neither the plain nor the symmetric variant. We read this as a
domain problem: pairs such as \emph{filaria}/\emph{nematoda} teach relations
between rare words, while the missed \NEG{} pairs in DiCo are between common
words used in context.

\subsection{The negative class}
Without the prior, the three reversible labels are rarely confused with each
other; the errors that remain are almost all \NEG{} against the rest. We tested
six ways to improve that decision (Table~\ref{tab:neg}); the sixth, WordNet
relation pairs, is discussed with the new backbones above.

Class weighting (weight 2 on \NEG) and focal loss ($\gamma=2$) lowered HardCons
on mDeBERTa by 3.9 and 1.1 points. Reverse-negative augmentation gained 0.8 F1
points on mDeBERTa but cost 4.7 SoftCons points, and hurt XLM-R-large on every
metric. We then added 1{,}010 chunk pairs from the iSTS 2016
answers-students data, mapping EQUI to \EQ, SPE1 and SPE2 to \FE{} and \BE, and
SIMI, REL and OPPO to \NEG. We did not use the headlines and images subsets,
because they are the source of PhrasIS and contain its test pairs, and none of
the converted pairs occurs in the DiCo training or development data. The extra
data lowered F1 for both backbones, and a second mDeBERTa seed was also
worse.

The last method was a stacked detector. A logistic regression and a
gradient-boosted classifier predict \NEG{} from the single-row log-probabilities
of the seven ensemble members and from lexical features (token overlap,
containment, length, number mismatch, negation words), with five-fold
cross-validation grouped by \texttt{pair\_id}. The best setting raised F1 by
0.6 points but lowered SoftCons by 3.9 and HardCons by 3.3. The ensemble's own
\NEG{} score seems to carry the same lexical signal already.

Moving the \NEG{} threshold $\beta$ only trades one score for another
(Fig.~\ref{fig:bias}). Lowering it towards $-1.5$ lifts HardCons to 90.3 and
SoftCons to 99.3 while F1 falls to 76.9; raising it does the reverse. The mean
of the three scores stays between 88 and 89 over the whole range.

\subsection{Error analysis}
\label{sec:errors}
To see why, we pooled the clean-7 log-probabilities per source pair and
inspected the 383 T1 development pairs. The errors are one-sided: 48 of the 106
\NEG{} pairs are decoded as a reversible label (\NEG{} recall 45\%), against 13
of the 277 reversible pairs decoded as \NEG. The pooled \NEG{} score separates
the two groups with an AUC of 0.90 on T1 and 0.87 on T3, and the best
threshold on it gives a \NEG{} F1 of only 74.6. This is why $\beta$ cannot
help: the problem is the ranking, not the cut-off. The missed \NEG{} pairs are
mostly iSTS SIMI and REL relations that look like entailment, such as
\emph{a creek}/\emph{in a river}, \emph{38}/\emph{to 39} and
\emph{Australian senator}/\emph{Australian lawmaker}; telling a hypernym from a
sibling or a merely related phrase needs lexical knowledge that the phrases
alone do not carry. Some labels also depend on the sentence the chunks came
from, which DiCo does not provide: with all language views pooled, 27 of the
383 source pairs (7.0\%) are missed by all eighteen multilingual clean runs,
whatever the backbone or loss.

Among the reversible labels the picture is different. Given the prior, the
ensemble picks the right one of \EQ, \FE{} and \BE{} for 92.1\% of T1 pairs
and 88.8\% of T3 pairs. Direction is essentially solved: only 2 of the 22 T1
errors confuse \FE{} with \BE; the rest confuse \EQ{} with one of them, a
question of granularity. T3 adds 13 \BE-as-\EQ{} errors, so Basque loses
specificity. Finally, the ensemble members make similar mistakes: the error
sets of mDeBERTa and XLM-R-large overlap with a Jaccard index of 0.61, and
those of two mDeBERTa seeds with 0.69, which limits what further seeds of the
same backbones can add.



\subsection{Hierarchical composition and ensemble selection}
\label{sec:hierresults}
Table~\ref{tab:systems} lists the systems we packaged as development
submissions. The hierarchical composition helps without any selection. With
the clean-7 ensemble as the gate and eleven prior-aware models as the direction
ensemble, it reaches 81.4 / 96.8 / 89.5, against 80.8 / 95.7 / 88.0 for
clean-7 alone, with higher HardCons on all four tracks (T1 F1 is unchanged). Since both member lists are fixed
in advance, this is the estimate we trust most for unseen data. Adding the
WordNet and reranker models to the gate lowers it slightly (81.3 / 96.2 /
89.0).

Greedy selection per track adds more on the development set. Without the
prior, selecting gate and direction members jointly gives 83.7 / 98.6 / 91.7,
which is 2.9 F1 and 3.7 HardCons points above clean-7 and above the
leaderboard leader on HardCons, though still 7.7 F1 points behind it. With the
prior, per-track selection reaches 94.3 / 100 / 93.1, ahead of the leader on
every track. The selections pick odd members: mmBERT and the reverse-negative
XLM-R model, both weak on their own, appear in some tracks, and the chosen
sets change when more candidates are added. That instability is the usual sign
of fitting the development set, so we expect selected systems to lose part of
their margin on test data.



\subsection{Cross-track pooling}
Because a \texttt{pair\_id} is shared by all tracks, decoding the four
development files together pools up to eighteen views of each source pair. In
a four-model ensemble with the prior this added about one point of both F1
and HardCons over per-track decoding (92.7 / 100 / 91.3 against
91.8 / 100 / 90.3). It also uses the other tracks' inputs to predict a
given track and makes the four tracks' predictions identical, so we leave it
out of the reported results until the organizers say whether it is allowed.

\section{Discussion}

Two caveats apply to every number above. First, all model, ensemble and
threshold choices were made on the development set, which has 277 reversible
pairs per track; the standard error of HardCons at that size is about 2.6
points, so differences of one or two points between single runs are within
noise, and the reported scores are optimistic. Grouped cross-validation over
the training and development data would give firmer estimates. Second, the
lead over the leaderboard depends entirely on the structural prior. Without
it, our best selected system is 7.7 points of F1 behind and our best fixed
system 10.0 points, and the gap is the \NEG{} decision. Selected ensembles
are also the least trustworthy numbers in this report: their member lists
change when candidates are added, and several members are weak on their own.
For the evaluation phase we would submit fixed ensembles, such as
\texttt{hier\_fixed}, trained on training and development data together.

The negative results are consistent with what is known about NLI models on
short, lexically overlapping inputs~\cite{hans2019,glockner2018breaking}: the
hard \NEG{} cases are phrases that are related, similar or opposite, and more
weight, more data of the same kind, WordNet relations between rare words or
extra surface features did not teach the models to separate them. Two
further directions stay within our model budget: lexical-relation pairs drawn
from the vocabulary of DiCo itself rather than the whole wordnet, and grouped
cross-validation over training and development data to select ensembles on
held-out folds instead of on the development set. Methods that change the model's inductive bias rather
than its training signal, such as larger instruction-tuned models used as
teachers, are the obvious next step.

\section{Conclusion}

Consistency in DiCo-NLI can be guaranteed by decoding the two directions of a
pair together, which raises SoftCons and HardCons at no training cost. With
that in place, the task reduces to deciding whether a phrase pair stands in an
entailment relation at all, and the evidence we collected says this decision
is limited by lexical knowledge about common words in context. Splitting the
work between a negative-class gate and a direction model trained only on the
entailment labels is the one change that helped without selection, lifting the
fixed clean ensemble to 81.4 / 96.8 / 89.5 on the development set. Selected
ensembles reach 83.7 / 98.6 / 91.7 without and 94.3 / 100 / 93.1 with the
layout-based prior. Whether that prior may be used in the evaluation phase is
a question for the organizers.
